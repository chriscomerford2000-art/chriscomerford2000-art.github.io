    Structure an essay
    • Introduction. Answer the question straight away and say what you will argue. Two to four sentences is enough.
    • Body paragraphs. One main idea each. Use Point, Evidence, Explain, Link: make your point, back it up, explain why it matters, then tie it back to the question.
    • Conclusion. Sum up your argument in fresh words. Don't bring in new evidence.
    Commonly confused words
    their, there, they're
    Their means belonging to them. There is a place, or "there is". They're means "they are".
    its, it's
    Its means belonging to it. It's means "it is" or "it has".
    your, you're
    Your means belonging to you. You're means "you are".
    affect, effect
    Affect is usually a verb: to change something. Effect is usually a noun: the result.
    then, than
    Then is about time or order. Than is for comparing.
    to, too, two
    To is a direction or part of a verb. Too means "also" or "excessively". Two is the number.
    who, whom
    Who does the action ("Who wrote it?"). Whom receives it ("To whom did you write?").
    fewer, less
    Fewer for things you can count. Less for amounts you can't.
    lose, loose
    Lose means to misplace or not win. Loose means not tight.
    accept, except
    Accept means to receive or agree. Except means "apart from".
    principal, principle
    Principal means main, or the head of a school. A principle is a rule or belief.
    whose, who's
    Whose shows ownership. Who's means "who is" or "who has".
    Punctuation rules
    Comma
    Use it after an opening phrase, between items in a list, around extra information, and before a joining word like "but" when it links two full sentences. Don't separate a subject from its verb.
    Comma splice
    Two full sentences joined by only a comma is an error. Use a full stop, a semicolon, or a joining word instead.
    Semicolon
    Joins two closely related full sentences. It can also separate items in a long list.
    Colon
    Comes after a complete sentence to introduce a list, an explanation, or a quotation.
    Apostrophe
    Shows that letters are missing (don't) or that something belongs to someone (the dog's bone, the dogs' bones). Never use it to make a plural: "apples", not "apple's".
    Quotation marks
    Put the exact words you are quoting inside them. Keep quotes short and fit them into your own sentence.
    Linking words
    Adding a point
    also, furthermore, in addition, moreover
    Contrasting
    however, on the other hand, although, whereas, nevertheless
    Giving a reason or result
    because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently
    Giving an example
    for instance, such as, in particular
    Showing order
    firstly, next, then, finally
    Concluding
    in conclusion, overall, to sum up
    Writing about a quotation or text
    1. Introduce the quotation in your own words so the reader knows where it comes from.
    2. Keep it short. Quote only the words that prove your point.
    3. Explain what it shows and the effect it has on the reader. Don't just repeat it.
    Persuasive writing techniques
    • Direct address. Speaking to the reader as "you".
    • Rhetorical question. A question asked for effect, not for an answer.
    • Rule of three. Three examples or ideas in a row.
    • Repetition. Repeating a word or phrase to stress it.
    • Facts and statistics. Evidence that makes the argument harder to dismiss.
    • Emotive language. Words chosen to make the reader feel something.
    • Anecdote. A short true story that makes the issue personal.
    • Counter-argument. Admitting the other side, then showing why yours is stronger.
    Check your work
    • Read it aloud. You will hear missing words and clumsy sentences.
    • Does every sentence have a subject and a verb, and make sense on its own?
    • Do names, places and the word "I" start with a capital letter?
    • Does each paragraph have one main idea, and does it answer the question?
    • Look for words you have used too often and swap some for different ones.
